## Evidence frame

- DCI evaluates whether performers maintain a uniform marching style, handle movement demands, and
  coordinate drill and choreography with musical performance. [DCI Judging
  101](https://www.dci.org/news/dci-judging-101/)
- A published collegiate survey found that 25% of 1,379 marching-band and color-guard participants
  reported a marching-related musculoskeletal injury; 87.7% of those injuries involved the lower
  extremity. This supports attention to lower-body capacity and progressive loading, but it does
  not show that straight-leg technique caused the injuries. [Medical Problems of Performing
  Artists study](https://doi.org/10.21091/mppa.2015.2018)
- Instrument load can affect posture and stability. Small studies found greater trunk and shoulder
  muscle activity with a heavier marching euphonium posture and changes in anterior-posterior
  postural stability while carrying an instrument.
  [Posture study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24259926/);
  [stability study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27575294/)
- Marching-band performers experience meaningful exertional-heat risk. This makes acclimatization,
  workload progression, environmental modification, hydration, rest, and emergency procedures
  part of physical preparation rather than separate administrative details.
  [Core-temperature study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8010928/);
  [heat-illness risk-factor study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11726051/)
- Post-activity static stretching can support flexibility and range of motion. Current evidence does
  **not** justify claiming that it reliably prevents soreness, restores strength, or accelerates
  next-day recovery compared with passive recovery.
  [Systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8133317/)

## Comparable straight-leg technique descriptions

These sources are not replacements for Dynamic Marching; they help establish that the physical
demands being considered are characteristic of the broader technique family.

- Music City Drum Corps describes forward and backward straight/long-leg technique alongside
  dance, performance, and physical wellness.
  [2025 audition material](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5fac802bd1200b5ef882ec8b/t/66fbec16163ffe769104662c/1727786009358/Tuba%2B%281%29.pdf)
- Midwestern State University describes a modified straight-leg forward technique with a powerful
  first-step push, toes raised, heel contact, continuous heel-to-toe transfer, rhythmic foot
  passage, and a controlled upper body. Its backward technique keeps performers on the platforms
  with the upper body still.
  [Marching-band handbook](https://directory.msutexas.edu/storage/files/msu_marching_band_handbook-20190910-142516.pdf)
- Stevenson University describes stacked posture, knees aligned but **not locked**, backward travel
  on the forefoot/platform, and slides in which the feet travel while the shoulders, head, and
  instrument remain oriented toward the audience.
  [Technique guide](https://bands.stevenson.edu/wp-content/uploads/The-SU-Marching-Technique-Guide.8.15.23.docx.pdf)
- Eclipse Drum & Bugle Corps describes an elevated upper body with core and lat engagement and,
  again, knees straight but not locked.
  [Brass packet](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/63397265e2571e10e7c29313/t/6524e11e6d13d0343e1da1aa/1696915743162/2024%2BBrass%2BAudition%2BPacket.pdf)

## Technique-to-physical-demand map

The technique descriptions are sourced; the capacity connections in this table are informed
biomechanical inferences to be reviewed with a qualified athletic trainer or physical therapist
before becoming a formal student program.

| Visual demand | Plausibly required capacities | Particular preparation questions |
|---|---|---|
| Stacked posture while moving and playing | Trunk endurance; lumbopelvic control; thoracic extension; scapular and neck endurance; breathing under isometric load | Can students preserve alignment without bracing excessively, arching, or locking knees? |
| Instrument carriage | Section-specific shoulder, upper-back, rotator-cuff, arm, wrist, grip, trunk, and back endurance; balance under external load | Should carriage preparation differ for flute, brass, battery, sousaphone, and equipment roles? |
| Mark time | Single-leg balance; hip-flexor control; stance-leg and pelvic stability; ankle/calf control; rhythmic coordination | Can students lift and replace the foot without pelvic collapse, trunk motion, or timing change? |
| Straight-leg forward roll-through | Ankle dorsiflexion and toes-up endurance; controlled plantar flexion; foot intrinsic control; hip flexor/hamstring coordination; gluteal and quadriceps control; single-leg balance | How do we teach “long/straight” without knee locking or forced hyperextension? |
| Backward on the platforms | Calf/soleus and Achilles-region endurance; foot and ankle stability; hip-extension and posterior-chain control; balance and spatial awareness | Does fatigue cause heels to collapse, step size to shorten, posture to lean, or timing to change? |
| Slides and upper/lower-body separation | Hip rotation; lateral hip and pelvic control; adductor/abductor capacity; trunk anti-rotation or controlled rotation; thoracic rotation; ankle control along the path | Can shoulders square without transferring forced rotation into knees or feet? |
| Starts, halts, direction and effort changes | Eccentric braking in hips, thighs, and calves; lateral knee/hip control; ankle control; rapid balance reorganization; reacceleration capacity | Can the student absorb and redirect force without knee collapse, extra steps, or upper-body disruption? |
| Body movement and choreography | Task-specific multiplanar mobility, floor-transfer ability, coordination, strength, and the ability to recover cleanly to playing posture | The actual movement vocabulary must be known before the conditioning demand can be specified. |
| Long repetitions and full visual blocks | Aerobic base; local muscular endurance; repeat-effort recovery; progressive load tolerance; heat acclimatization | Can technique quality remain stable as exertion, temperature, equipment load, and complexity rise? |

## Dynamic preparation candidate library

These are candidates to inspect and teach, not a prescribed sequence. A future routine should move
from general temperature-raising activity toward the exact marching task. Hospital for Special
Surgery recommends first raising body temperature with light-to-moderate movement, then using
controlled dynamic range-of-motion work before activity.
[HSS static and dynamic stretching guidance](https://www.hss.edu/health-library/move-better/static-dynamic-stretching)

### Temperature and task progression

- Brisk walk → easy march → controlled rehearsal cadence
- Short forward/backward/slide technique bouts with full recovery
- Low-amplitude direction changes, then gradually larger or faster changes
- Instrument-off movement before instrument-on repetition when appropriate

### Ankles, calves, feet, and lower-leg control

- Ankle or knee-over-toe rocks
- Heel walks and toe walks
- Double-leg heel raises, later progressing toward offset or supported single-leg work
- Toe or tibialis raises / light-band dorsiflexion
- Ankle inversion and eversion control with light resistance
- Towel curls, toe pickups, and other gentle intrinsic-foot work
- Single-leg balance with support available, later adding head or free-leg movement

The [AAOS foot and ankle conditioning
library](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/foot-and-ankle-conditioning-program/) contains
clinician-reviewed examples for calf, shin, foot, toe, ankle, and balance work. It is a
rehabilitation-oriented library, not a marching prescription.

### Hips, thighs, and movement range

- Forward/backward leg swings
- Side-to-side leg swings
- Open/close-the-gate hip movements
- Hamstring sweeps
- Controlled straight-leg or “toy soldier” walks
- Walking or reverse lunges
- Lateral lunges
- Inchworms
- Low-amplitude 90/90 hip switches

### Strength, balance, and control

- Tempo squat
- Heel raise with reach
- Hip hike
- Split squat or reverse lunge
- Step-up and controlled step-down
- Supported single-leg Romanian-deadlift pattern
- Glute bridge
- Side-lying hip abduction or clamshell
- Single-leg balance with marching free-leg motion

The [AAOS hip](https://orthoinfo.org/recovery/hip-conditioning-program/) and
[knee](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) libraries offer examples and
target-area descriptions. Their clinical dosage should not be copied automatically into camp.

### Trunk, posture, and instrument support

- Bird dog
- Front plank and modified side plank
- Controlled torso rotation
- Scapular wall slides
- Controlled rows or light carries
- Section-specific instrument-position holds and movement, progressed cautiously

The [AAOS spine conditioning
library](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/globalassets/pdfs/spine-conditioning-program.pdf) provides
examples of bird dog, plank, side plank, bridge, and related trunk work.

### Aerobic and repeated-effort candidates

- Continuous easy-to-moderate marching
- Short technique-integrated cadence intervals
- Forward/backward/slide circuits
- Short halt, restart, and direction-change intervals
- Alternating instrument-on and instrument-off work
- Talk-test or simple perceived-exertion monitoring

The purpose would be to preserve technique under gradually increasing work, not to use conditioning
as punishment.

## Forte Athletics comparison

teaching architecture: learn from video, reference setup and corrections, then lead from a concise
field sheet.

| Forte sample movement | Likely straight-leg relevance |

| Heel raise with reach | Direct overlap with calf endurance, tall posture, ankle/foot control, and backward platform technique |
| Hip hike | Frontal-plane pelvic control, single-leg stance, lateral hip endurance, slides, and direction changes |

Candidate areas not visible in the **free sample**:

- dorsiflexor/toes-up endurance;
- foot intrinsic and ankle inversion/eversion control;
- unilateral stance and eccentric step-down control;
- hamstring and hip-extension capacity;
- trunk anti-rotation and lateral endurance for slides;
- balance progressions;
- instrument-specific upper-body endurance;
- technique-integrated aerobic progression; and
- comprehensive post-rehearsal floor mobility.

full paid program, which has not been reviewed.

## Floor-based lower-body cooldown and mobility library

These candidates fit Rob's preference for seated, supine, prone, or side-lying work in the second
half of the day. “Recovery” here means a deliberate cooldown and range-of-motion practice—not a
promise of reduced soreness or faster physiological recovery.

Exact hold times and repetitions vary across the clinical sources. Common examples range from
roughly 15–60 seconds, often repeated, but Ashley's dosage should be chosen later with qualified
guidance. All movements should be slow, self-controlled, non-bouncing, and free of pain.

| Area | Floor-based candidates | Source and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Quadriceps | Side-lying quadriceps stretch; prone quadriceps stretch; side-lying combined quad/hip-flexor stretch | Keep knees near one another and pelvis controlled; do not arch the back or force heel-to-buttock range. [NHS cooldown stretches](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/how-to-stretch-after-exercising/); [Cambridge University Hospitals](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/stretches-for-the-hip/) |
| Hamstrings | Supine hamstring stretch with hands behind the thigh; towel/strap variation; wall-supported supine stretch | Do not pull on the knee joint. A slight bend or reduced range may be appropriate; radiating pain, numbness, or tingling is a stop condition. [AAOS knee program](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/); [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/fitness/in-depth/stretching/art-20546848) |
| Hip flexors | Half-kneeling stretch with padding; side-lying hip-flexor/quad stretch | Use a gentle pelvic tuck and glute engagement instead of back arching. [HSS hip-flexor guide](https://www.hss.edu/health-library/move-better/hip-flexor-stretch) |
| Glutes and deep hip rotators | Supine figure four; knee-to-chest; seated 90/90; gentle seated rotation | Figure four and knee-to-chest appear easiest to standardize. The 90/90 position may not be accessible to everyone. [AAOS hip program](https://www.orthoinfo.org/recovery/hip-conditioning-program/); [HSS hip guide](https://www.hss.edu/health-library/move-better/hip-flexor-stretch) |
| Adductors and groin | Seated butterfly; reclined butterfly with optional support under knees; wide-seated gentle hinge; optional happy-baby position | Reclined butterfly is a lower-load option. Avoid forcing knees toward the ground. [NHS cooldown stretches](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/how-to-stretch-after-exercising/); [CUH hip stretches](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/stretches-for-the-hip/) |
| Lateral hip / abductors | Gentle figure-four or rotation may affect posterior-lateral tissues; no universal “IT-band floor stretch” selected | Cross-body adduction can aggravate diagnosed lateral-hip/gluteal-tendon pain. This area may be better addressed through strength and individual guidance. [CUH gluteal-tendinopathy guidance](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/gluteal-tendinopathy/) |
| Calf and Achilles region | Long-seated towel calf stretch with straight knee; possible bent-knee soleus variation after expert review | The straight-knee towel version is well sourced. The self-led bent-knee setup should be vetted before group use. [AAOS foot/ankle program](https://www.orthoinfo.org/recovery/foot-and-ankle-conditioning-program/); [HSS ankle guide](https://www.hss.edu/health-library/move-better/ankle-stretches-exercises) |
| Ankle and shin | Ankle alphabet; slow ankle pumps and circles; gentle top-of-foot/anterior-ankle stretch | These are mobility resets more than stretches. Avoid forced end range with pain, swelling, instability, or recent sprain. [AAOS foot/ankle program](https://www.orthoinfo.org/recovery/foot-and-ankle-conditioning-program/) |
| Feet, arches, and toes | Seated plantar-fascia/toe-extension stretch; gentle toe flexion/extension; seated ball or bottle roll | Condition-specific clinical dosages should not be imported automatically. Shared balls create equipment and hygiene questions. [AAOS plantar-fascia guidance](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/diseases--conditions/plantar-fasciitis-and-bone-spurs); [AAOS foot/ankle program](https://www.orthoinfo.org/recovery/foot-and-ankle-conditioning-program/) |
| Whole lower body / downshift | Supine knee-to-chest; reclined butterfly; gentle trunk rotation; supported child's pose where comfortable; slow breathing | These may help create a calm closing sequence, but comfort, modesty, surface temperature, and ability to get down/up safely matter. |

## Safety, age, and governance notes

- “Straight leg” must never become “locked or hyperextended knee.” Comparable technique guides
  explicitly distinguish the two.
- Youth resistance and bodyweight work can be appropriate when technique comes before load,
  progression is gradual, and qualified supervision is present.
  [American Academy of Pediatrics](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/145/6/e20201011/76942/Resistance-Training-for-Children-and-Adolescents)
- Heat acclimatization and total load must include rehearsal duration, intensity, equipment,
  clothing, surface, temperature, humidity, sleep, illness, and prior activity—not just the workout.
  [CDC athlete heat guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/heat-health/risk-factors/heat-and-athletes.html)
- Every movement needs an easier version and a meaningful rest/opt-out route without stigma.
- Floor stretching should be self-controlled. Student partners should not force limbs.
- Do not stretch through pain. Stop and refer when there is sharp or worsening pain, visible
  swelling or injury, limping, inability to use the limb normally, numbness or tingling, dizziness,
  chest pain, fainting, or heat-illness signs.
  [Boston Children's Health Physicians](https://bchp.childrenshospital.org/news/easing-kids-sports-when-push-through-and-when-take-break)
- Diagnosed injuries, recent surgery, persistent pain, instability/hypermobility, and medical
  restrictions require individualized qualified guidance.
- If a demanding performance block follows later in the day, prolonged static stretching should
  not automatically be placed immediately before it. The final schedule must account for what
  students must do next.
